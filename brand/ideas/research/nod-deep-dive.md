# Nod: deeper research

## How it's done today
- **Schools (Kenya).** Compliance guides tell schools to use detailed photo consent forms that name the platforms, the purpose and how long images are kept; to let parents agree to some uses but not others; to offer opt-outs at events (one guide suggests coloured wristbands); to keep a photo-consent database with easy lookup; and never to rely on blanket consent (Lake Site School compliance guide). In practice this means paper forms at admission.
- **The newest case.** In August 2026 the ODPC ordered Mukumu Girls to pay KES 300,000 after the school gave a pupil's admission photo to a newspaper during a health crisis in 2023. The ruling turned on *purpose*: the photo was collected for admission, so publishing it was a new, incompatible purpose. The school also ignored the regulator, so the Commissioner recommended prosecuting its board (fines up to KES 5M) (The Standard, Aug 2026). Lesson for Nod: consent has to be tied to a purpose, and changing the purpose needs a new yes.
- **Events.** The common opt-out is coloured lanyards (red, yellow, green), but they fail in practice: they're costly, people take them off, and the colours get lost in clothing. One conference switched to a single fluorescent green wristband that people take only if they *don't* want to be photographed. 1,500 of them cost about $42, and they're easy to spot in the viewfinder (Abstractions write-up). The ODPC recorded-media guidance also names stickers or a separate area as acceptable opt-outs.

**Design takeaway:** at a venue, an opt-out signal people can see (one bright colour) is simpler and more honest than asking every guest to opt in. Ask for opt-in consent where the stakes are higher: children, close-ups, and ads.

## What already exists (and what it costs)
| Tool | What it does | Price | Gap for our market |
|---|---|---|---|
| FairConsent (Fairpicture) | Digital consent for photographers, journalists, NGOs | $29–99/month | Built for photographers and NGOs, not venues or schools; no pre-post check |
| SpotMyPhotos | Event photo delivery: guests register (QR) and get their photos | $142–295/month | Delivery, not publication consent; priced for photo businesses |
| Premagic | Event photo delivery with face matching | not checked | Same: delivery at events |
| Easy Release and similar | Model-release forms on a phone | one-off app | One person at a time; no venue or school flow |

None of these combine consent per purpose, an opt-out signal at the venue, a check before each post and takedown on withdrawal, on WhatsApp, for everyday African venues and schools. That's the gap. It's a narrow one, so the real-world test matters.

## Price anchors (Nairobi)
- Social media management: about KES 25,000–35,000 a month basic, 50,000–150,000 mid-tier (SIIT, 2026).
- Data protection registration: KES 4,000, renewal KES 2,000 (ODPC).
- Fines and orders: KES 300,000 (Mukumu) to 4.55M (Roma School).

**Hypothesis to test, not a price:** Nod as an add-on to our social content retainer (a small fraction of a KES 25–35k monthly fee), or a standalone per-school or per-venue plan below FairConsent's $29. Test willingness to pay with real owners before setting any number.

## The paper test (for when Zayn picks five owners)
1. A one-page consent card: who is asking, the purpose (tonight's Instagram / our website / an ad), how long we keep it, how to withdraw. Tick boxes per purpose.
2. A sample green "please don't post me" wristband or sticker.
3. Three questions to ask: would you hand this out on a busy night? What would you pay to never think about it again? Who in your team would run it?
4. Count how many say yes to trying it for one night or one term.

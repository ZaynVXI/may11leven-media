# Competitor analysis: agencies like us

The founder's brief: "There are 1,000 new AI agencies daily. Be the one that stands apart. See what they're doing wrong and avoid it." His point that frames everything here: "No one cares about an AI wrapper. Anyone can vibe code them. A badly deployed AI can cost you your business."

## Three groups we get compared with

### 1. AI automation agencies (the flood)
Freelancers and small shops selling chatbots, "AI receptionists" and n8n, Make or Zapier workflows. Builds usually cost $1,500–15,000 and retainers $500–5,000 a month (Ciela's reported ranges; not independently checked).

**What they get wrong**
- **Wrappers, not systems.** They sell a chatbot or a single workflow that never connects to the business's real data. When the bot doesn't know, it guesses (Runframe).
- **Build and leave.** Automations break as APIs, prompts and processes change, and fixing them becomes another paid project (Runframe). One agency lost a $2,500-a-month client after a lead workflow failed silently for three days and about 40 leads were lost (Indie Hackers).
- **No failure plan.** Their pitches don't say what happens when the bot is wrong or attacked. The Air Canada, Chevrolet $1 Tahoe, DPD and Cursor cases show where that ends (see after-hours-guardrails.md).
- **"We work with any platform."** Spread thin, so they're slow to find what broke (Runframe).
- **Same template for every industry.** They miss sector rules and local reality, such as M-Pesa, WhatsApp-first guests and Swahili-English messages.
- **Hidden prices.** "Custom quote" lets them price on budget, and buyers can't compare (Runframe).
- **Efficiency pitches.** They sell "faster, cheaper". Clients want sales, customers and revenue. Digiday reports 70% of agencies say AI cut costs and 31% say it cost more than expected (Digiday, sponsored piece).
- **No design.** The interface is a default widget with a robot avatar, which tells the guest they're talking to something cheap.

### 2. Hotel chatbot software (the direct alternative for After hours)
HiJiffy (from $200 a month) and Asksuite (from $300 a month). Both connect to dozens of booking systems; prices beyond the entry tier are by quote (HotelTechReport).

**What reviewers say goes wrong** (HotelTechReport)
- Asksuite: struggles with complex or unusual requests and can give inaccurate answers; shallow reporting.
- HiJiffy: slow support, robotic replies, misses rephrased questions, gets stuck in loops; sync delays and setup trouble.
- Both: one product for every hotel, so the brand voice, local payments and the small independent hotel's reality come second.

**Our opening:** small independent hotels and guest houses in East Africa (Field Study 01: 51 Zanzibar hotels, 16% show a price, half send "Book" to another site). They need the website, the booking flow and a safe agent together. These tools sell only the chat.

### 3. Premium creative studios (our bar, not our enemy)
Motto, Holographik, Qclay, Zajno, .raw, Artemii Lebedev. Their craft is the standard we hold ourselves to.

**What they do well:** one big idea per brand (Motto's Docker work is built on one idea, "Run on Trust"), systems instead of single pieces, real research, beautiful presentation.
**Where they leave room for us**
- **AI is mostly inside the studio.** Motto's Brand Lab and Agent Hawkeye (an AI that checks design work) make the studio faster. They don't build and run the client's operational agents.
- **Few work in our first markets.** Mostly European and US clients at European and US prices.
- **Sameness creeps in.** Qclay's Paylane is polished, but the blue-gradient phone with a big balance ($117,450.17) and "We are open for new projects 👋" could come from many studios. Craft without a point of view starts to look generic, which is the same critique the founder gave our own work.

## How May11Leven Labs stands apart
**Positioning line (draft):** *We design it, and we make it safe to run.*
Brand, website, app and AI agent, built as one system, with the failure plan shown up front.

| They do | We do instead |
|---|---|
| Sell a chatbot | Sell the system around it: availability, rate table, payments, policy cards, a person in the loop |
| Say "AI does it all" | Show what our AI is **not allowed** to do, and why |
| Hide failure | Publish the failure modes and the red-team results for every agent we ship |
| Build and leave | Stay on: monitoring, alerts, re-testing after every update, a kill switch |
| "Custom quote" | Published starting prices (brand identity from $800 is already public) |
| Same template everywhere | Built for the place: M-Pesa, WhatsApp, local languages, slow phones |
| Default widget design | The agent's interface designed to the brand, accessible, readable on a cheap phone |
| Efficiency claims | Outcome claims we can measure: bookings kept, commission saved, replies after 18:00 |
| Posts of pretty mockups | Posts that answer the hard question (the next After hours post: "Try to get it for free") |

## What we must never do (the list to check every post and offer against)
1. Never post an AI concept without showing how it fails safely.
2. Never claim a result we haven't measured. Concepts are labelled "concept".
3. Never let a model set a price, confirm a payment or invent a policy in anything we build.
4. Never hand over a build without monitoring and a named person to call.
5. Never ship a default-looking chat widget.
6. Never compete on being cheapest. As BairesDev's founder put it, underpricing "more often signals uncertainty rather than good value" (Crunchbase).
7. Never use "AI-powered" as the headline. The outcome is the headline; the AI is the method.

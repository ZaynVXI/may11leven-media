# AI Design OS: how to design AI products

## Six paradigms (pick the one the product really is)
| Paradigm | Examples | The user wants to | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conversational | chatbots, voice | ask and explore | made-up answers, slow back-and-forth |
| Agentic and assistive | copilots, agents | hand off the work | it does the wrong thing, user loses control |
| Command-based | buttons, filters, short prompts | instruct and get it done | no good fallback, vague commands |
| Co-creation and generative | image, text, video tools | create, remix, iterate | fatigue, unclear authorship |
| Ambient and contextual | quiet nudges in the background | be noticed and reminded | privacy, control |
| Generative UI | screens built on the fly from intent and data | get exactly the screen they need | consistency |

## Twelve principles
1. Put people first.
2. Keep people in control.
3. Open the black box (show what it's doing and why).
4. Design for the mess (wrong answers, partial results, retries).
5. Be honest about confidence.
6. Make the model learnable (people should learn what it can and can't do).
7. Co-create, don't dictate.
8. Protect what matters (data, money, reputation).
9. Know what not to automate.
10. Design for everyone, with everyone.
11. Expect variability (the same request can give different answers).
12. Give context, not just answers.

## How we use it
- Every AI agent or automation we design starts by naming its paradigm. A WhatsApp booking agent is conversational and agentic; a lead-sorting automation is ambient.
- For each screen, check principles 2, 3, 4 and 9: the owner can stop or correct it, sees what it did, has a path when it fails, and the risky steps (payments, refunds, replies to angry guests) stay with a person.
- Waiting states: use thinking-orbs (brand/tools/thinking-orbs) with an honest label of what it's doing.
